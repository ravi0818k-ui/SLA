HOW LONG 100 PAGES TAKE

A 100-page book is not a mountain

A typical page holds about 250 words, so 100 pages is about 25,000 words. An average adult reads non-fiction silently at about 238 words a minute. That's about 1 hour 45 minutes for the whole book, at a normal pace.

So most people don't need superhuman speed. They need to stop losing time: re-reading lines, drifting off, checking the phone, and forgetting it all a week later.

How long will your book take?

Reading time: about 1 h 45 min · at 20 minutes a day: 6 days

Don't know your speed? Take the 2-minute test in Exercise 1; your result fills in here.

WHAT SPEED READING IS

Your eyes jump. They don't glide.

When you read, your eyes make quick jumps along the line and stop for about a quarter of a second on a word. Reading happens during those stops. At each stop you see only a few letters sharply, so no training lets you take in a whole page at a glance.

Real speed reading means wasting fewer stops: fewer jumps back over lines you already read, fewer moments of drifting off, and knowing which parts deserve a slow read and which can be skimmed.

"Read 1,000+ words a minute with full understanding"More speed past a point means less understanding. Choose the right speed for the text

THE 4-STEP METHOD

Preview, Ask, Pace, Recall

Use it on a chapter, an article or a whole book. The first two steps take five minutes and save you much more.

  1. 1

    Preview 5 min

    Read the title, headings, bold words, pictures, the summary and the questions at the end. Now you know the map before the journey.

  2. 2

    Ask 1 min

    Write 2 or 3 questions you want answered. Reading to find answers keeps your mind from drifting.

  3. 3

    Pace the read

    Move a pen or finger steadily under the line, a little faster than comfortable. Don't go back unless the meaning is lost. Slow down for hard parts.

  4. 4

    Recall 3 min

    Close the book. Write or say everything you remember and answer your questions. Then check what you missed.

TRY IT NOW

3 speed-reading exercises

Do them in order. Read the way you normally do: the goal is an honest starting point, not a high score.

Why we forget, and how to stop it

In the 1880s, a German psychologist named Hermann Ebbinghaus ran an unusual experiment on himself. He memorised long lists of nonsense syllables, short made-up words like DAX or BOK, so that nothing he already knew could help him. Then he tested himself after different gaps, from twenty minutes to a month. The results surprised him. A large part of each list was gone within the first day. After that, forgetting slowed down, but it never quite stopped. We now call this pattern the forgetting curve.

The curve explains a feeling every student knows. You read a chapter on Monday, understand all of it, and by Thursday it feels as if you never opened the book. Nothing is wrong with your brain. Forgetting is how memory clears space for what matters.

The good news is that the curve can be bent. Each time you pull an idea back out of memory, without looking at the page, the memory becomes stronger and fades more slowly. Researchers call this retrieval practice. It works best when the gaps grow: a quick review the same day, another a day or two later, then one after a week. This is called spaced repetition.

Notice what does not help much: reading the same page again and again. Re-reading feels productive, because the words look familiar, but familiarity is not the same as memory. The real test is whether you can explain the idea with the book closed.

So the next time you finish a chapter, close the book. Write down everything you remember in two minutes. Then check what you missed. That small, slightly uncomfortable effort is the difference between reading a chapter and keeping it.

Exercise 2

Pacer drill

The highlight moves three words at a time. Let your eyes follow it without going back. Start at your test speed, then try 25 words a minute faster. If you lose the meaning, slow down.

Your eyes do not glide smoothly across a line. They jump. Each jump is called a saccade, and between jumps the eyes stop for about a quarter of a second. These stops are called fixations, and they are when the actual reading happens. During a fixation you can make out only about seven or eight letters to the right of where you are looking, and fewer to the left. Everything further away is a blur.

Roughly one jump in ten goes backwards. These are regressions. Some are useful, because you go back when a sentence did not make sense. Many are just habit, or a sign that your mind wandered and you lost your place.

A pacer helps here. When you move a pen or finger steadily under the line, your eyes have something to follow, and they are less likely to slip back without a reason. Set the pacer a little faster than feels comfortable, but never so fast that you lose the meaning. Understanding is the point. Speed is only useful if the meaning comes with it.

On paper: use a pen cap or your finger the same way. A card held above the line, covering what you've read, also stops you slipping back.

Exercise 3

2-minute preview drill

Pick any chapter from your textbook or a book you're reading. Start the timer and look at only these, in this order:

  1. Chapter title and every heading
  2. Pictures, diagrams, tables and their captions
  3. Bold and highlighted words
  4. The first sentence of each paragraph
  5. The summary and the questions at the end

2:00

When the time is up, close the book and write 3 things the chapter is about. Then read it properly: you'll notice you go faster, because your brain already has the map.

THE HALF EVERYONE SKIPS

How to remember what you read

Reading fast is pointless if it's gone by next week. Without review, a large part of what you read fades within a day. That's normal: it's how memory works. These six habits bend that curve.

📝

Recall before you close the tab

Right after reading, write or say everything you remember with the book shut. Pulling it out of memory is what makes it stay.

❓

Read with questions

The questions you wrote in step 2 become hooks. Answers to questions you care about stick better than facts you skimmed past.

🗣️

Explain it simply

Explain the idea as if to a younger student, in your own words. Where you get stuck is exactly what you haven't understood yet.

📆

Review on a schedule

Same day, after 1–2 days, after a week. Each short review takes less time than the last, and the memory lasts longer.

🧭

Draw a one-page map

Put the main idea in the middle and branch out. Linking new ideas to what you already know makes them easier to find later. Try our free Mind Maps tool →

📵

One thing at a time

Phone in another room, 25-minute reading blocks. A memory needs your full attention while it's being made.

7-DAY PRACTICE PLAN

15–20 minutes a day for one week

Use a real book you want to read. Take Exercise 1 again on day 7 and compare with your own day-1 number.

  1. Day 1Take the reading-speed test. Note your speed and your score.
  2. Day 2Preview drill on one chapter, then read it. Recall for 3 minutes.
  3. Day 3Pacer drill at your test speed, then read 10 pages with a pen as pacer.
  4. Day 4Pacer at +25 wpm. Review day 2's chapter from memory first.
  5. Day 5Full method on a new chapter: Preview, Ask, Pace, Recall.
  6. Day 6Explain one chapter aloud in 2 minutes. Draw a one-page map.
  7. Day 7Take the test again. Did your speed and your score hold up?

Read it once. Now learn to keep it.

FAQ

Speed reading questions

Can anyone really read 1,000 words a minute?

Not with full understanding. Research on eye movements shows a trade-off: past a point, more speed means less understanding. At those speeds people are skimming, which is useful, but it is not the same as reading.

Should I stop saying the words in my head?

No. The inner voice is part of how we understand language, and trying to switch it off completely tends to hurt understanding. It naturally gets quieter and quicker with practice.

Is speed reading good for textbooks and board exam study?

Use a fast gear for previewing a chapter and a slow gear for the actual learning. For exams, what you can recall matters far more than how fast you read, so spend the time you save on testing yourself.

Does this work for Hindi books?

Yes. Previewing, reading with a question, using a pacer and recalling after reading work in any language. Your words-per-minute number will be different in each language, so compare yourself only with yourself.

How long until I read faster?

Most of the early gain comes from fewer re-reads and less mind-wandering, which you can feel within a week of daily practice. Long-term speed grows with how much you read and how many words you know.
